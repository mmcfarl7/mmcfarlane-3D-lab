## Research

- Spiral Design Process: This isn't something that is taken literally, it's sort of a symbolism where one design leads to the next and then the risks are analyzed, the prototype is evaluated, then another design is made to improve upon that one, then you do the risk analysis and evaluation all over again, and then you make another model after that one and so on and so forth "spiraling" down the near infinite design process until you reach a point where little to no improvements can be made without it being unethical or unnecessary as the final improvements would be near impossible to detect.

- FDM Anisotropy: This is the material's strength depending on the direction of the force is applied. For example, in 3D printing with various filaments, a model can be printed layer by layer and these layers have a powerful strength that can resist forces applied normal to which the layers are printed. However, if a force is applied through shear force, the layers are only as strong as the thermal bonds. This is what Anisotropy is, it's the variations of strength by the direction of the applied force.

- SLA shrinkage: This is caused by washing and curing a resin print. It's kind of like what clay and ceramics go through when they are heated or cured, they shrink very slightly which can be a problem when designs are created with precise measurements in mind like snap fits or just tight tolerances in general. Shrinking varies with the time and resin material, but usually is around 2-3% and this can be accounted for by simply finishing a model and scaling it up 2-3%.

- Laser Kerf: A laser kerf is when a laser printer trails along the border of the desired cut shape, leading to a shrinkage in the desired cut piece, which is rather small but can occasionally lead to failures of fits. A typical kerf ranges depending on the size of the border, but can be anywhere from 0.08 mm to 0.25 mm according to some resources.
